How bridge relayers actually work

By Mara Velez Sep 4, 2026 3 min read

Every bridge transaction depends on a relayer, but the term covers several very different jobs, and the differences decide who can halt your funds. A relayer is the off-chain actor who watches a deposit event on the source chain, then submits the corresponding proof or message to the destination chain so the bridge contract can release the wrapped assets. Without one, your transaction sits in limbo: the source chain shows a completed deposit, but the destination chain never mints anything.

Who actually runs the relayers?

Operators range from the bridge team itself to independent "solver" networks that compete to deliver your message. In optimistic bridges, relayers submit the message and then wait through a challenge window during which watchers can dispute it. In fraud-proof systems, the relayer is not the trust anchor—the watcher is. The relayer proposes state; the watcher verifies it. If you only look at the operator's identity, you are missing the more important question: what happens if the relayer and the watcher are the same entity? Some bridges mitigate this with external staking requirements for watchers, but stake size is not the same as independence.

What exactly does a relayer post on-chain?

For a typical lock-and-mint flow, the relayer submits the block header, the Merkle proof that your deposit transaction is included in that header, and the message payload. The destination contract verifies that the header was finalized by the source chain's light client—not just that the relayer says so. That finality check is where many bridges diverge. Some accept a single confirmation; others wait for the source chain's finality gadget. A relayer that posts an un-finalized header can be forced to re-submit, and during that window, your capital is neither here nor there. This is not a theoretical edge case; it is a routine occurrence during source-chain reorganizations.

How do relayers get paid?

Most relayers are not altruists. They collect a fee from the user's transaction—that fee is set by the destination chain's gas market and the bridge's own fee schedule—and in some designs, they also capture arbitrage between the source and destination liquidity pools. The incentive problem is structural:

  • If fees are too low, only a handful of relayers bother to run full nodes for every connected chain, and they can coordinate to keep fees high.
  • If competition is too fierce, relayers will cut corners on verification—posting proofs before they check the source chain's actual state—because the losing relayer eats the gas cost.
  • If a relayer controls a large share of message flow, it can reorder transactions for its own arbitrage benefit, a form of miner-extractable value that users pay for indirectly.
  • During congestion, relayers naturally prioritize high-fee transactions, so your small transfer may wait while bigger flows clear.

You can see this in action on any bridge's mempool: when gas spikes, low-fee messages stall. The failed transaction is not a bug; it is the relayer acting rationally.

What happens when a relayer fails?

Users expect the bridge to handle it, but the reality is uglier. If the relayer's submit transaction reverts due to an invalid proof, the event is simply dropped. Some bridges will pick it up again; many will not, because the relayer can't distinguish between an invalid proof and a stale one. The user must manually re-trigger the transaction, and the bridge's UI rarely surfaces that path. A liquidity provider on a bridge like LI FI sees the same symptom—pending state—while the actual matching engine tries different route combinations across relayers, each attempt costing gas. The protocol's robustness is a function of how many relayers serve the same route, not how many chains the bridge supports.

"A bridge with one relayer is a custodian that happens to use smart contracts. The code doesn't save you from a single point of failure; it just makes the failure transparent."

The practical test is simple: name your bridge, then name its relayer set. If the answer is "team-operated" or "one entity," the decentralized-settlement narrative collapses. Relay networks like the Witness network (used by LayerZero) and external solver markets (used by across-chain protocols) exist precisely because users learned that asking who relays is the first risk question, not the last.
