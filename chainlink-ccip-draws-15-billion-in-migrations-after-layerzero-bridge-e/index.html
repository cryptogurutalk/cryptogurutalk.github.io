Chainlink CCIP draws $15 billion in migrations after LayerZero bridge exploit

Chainlink CCIP has won the first major post-exploit vote on bridge security: BitGo’s August 4 decision to move WBTC from LayerZero pushed announced…

By Mara Velez Sep 4, 2026 2 min read

Chainlink CCIP has won the first major post-exploit vote on bridge security: BitGo’s August 4 decision to move WBTC from LayerZero pushed announced migrations to roughly $14.6 billion—commonly rounded to $15 billion—because issuers no longer accept a cross-chain route that can collapse to one verifier.

BitGo plans to standardize WBTC and future BitGo-issued assets on CCIP, calling the protocol its exclusive cross-chain provider. WBTC accounted for about $7.4 billion when the move was reported; BitGo valued it above $7.7 billion in its August 4 announcement. The rest comes from previously announced switches involving Mantle, KelpDAO, Lombard, Solv Protocol, Virtuals, Re and Kraken. This is value covered by infrastructure decisions, not $15 billion already transferred through CCIP.

What broke LayerZero’s security case?

The KelpDAO rsETH bridge failed because its Unichain-to-Ethereum route gave one LayerZero Labs-operated Decentralized Verifier Network sole authority to approve messages. On April 18, an attacker sent a forged packet claiming rsETH had been burned on Unichain; no burn occurred, yet the Ethereum adapter released 116,500 rsETH worth approximately $292 million.

The attacker had compromised LayerZero’s internal RPC environment after socially engineering a developer, poisoned two internal nodes and disrupted an external RPC provider. The sole DVN then produced a valid attestation from false chain data. With a 1-of-1 threshold and no optional verifier, Ethereum accepted packet 308 even though Unichain’s outbound nonce remained at 307, according to the Aave incident analysis.

LayerZero says KelpDAO changed an earlier 2-of-2 configuration and that the protocol executed exactly what the application specified. That defense exposes the defect: configurable security let a bridge carrying nearly $300 million choose one signer, while LayerZero Labs operated that signer. LayerZero has since stopped its DVN from serving as the sole required attestor.

Why is CCIP taking the migrations?

CCIP replaces the optional-verifier bargain with a stronger default. BitGo says every bridge lane uses at least 16 independent, security-reviewed node operators, supplemented by separate risk monitoring and rate limits intended to contain abnormal transfers. Under Chainlink’s Cross-Chain Token model, BitGo retains ownership of WBTC contracts and control over transfer parameters rather than surrendering its token administration to the bridge.

Who inherits the remaining risk?

Issuers receive standardized routing, rate controls and continued authority over mint-and-burn deployments; token holders inherit dependence on Chainlink’s operator set, risk network and the issuer’s own contract administration. That is still concentrated infrastructure risk, but it is materially harder to corrupt than the single-signature path that emptied KelpDAO’s adapter. The migration wave is not proof that CCIP cannot fail. It is proof that one configurable verifier is no longer an acceptable default for multibillion-dollar assets.
